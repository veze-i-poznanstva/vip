## The problem

Corruption needs trust. Someone who breaks the rules has to rely on the people around them not to
talk, and the safest choice is someone already bound to them: a relative, a friend, a schoolmate,
a party colleague. In Serbia a kum, a cousin or a school friend can all belong to such a circle.
Loyalty does the work that a contract cannot.

Corruption and favoritism rarely leave a clean money trail. Bribes are disguised as consulting
fees, assets are held in relatives' names or offshore companies, and much favoritism is not even
illegal: a contract steered to a friend's company, a cousin appointed to a board, a judge doing a
favor for a lawyer he knows. These circles show up as jobs given to connections, public tenders
won by the right company, and kickbacks. Sometimes the tie is the problem in itself, as when a job
goes to an official's wife. More often it sheds light on a world of relationships that is otherwise
hard to see.

"Follow the money" works when investigators can subpoena bank records, but journalists and
citizens usually cannot. What is often public, though scattered across many sources, is who is
connected to whom: who sits on which board, who owns shares where, who is related to whom, who
used to work where. Each fact on its own looks harmless. The problem is that nobody puts them
together.

This is not only a problem of countries seen as corrupt. In Denmark, which tops the corruption
rankings, firms with family ties to local politicians gained when those politicians gained power,
especially firms that depend on public demand [1]. Personal networks can pay even where everyday
bribery is rare.

## Following the connections

Platforms like Poderopedia gather scattered public facts into one map of people, companies and
institutions and the links between them. With everything in one place, patterns appear that no
single record shows.

## Why it is valuable

- **Connections are worth real money.** Stock prices of Indonesian firms tied to Suharto fell more
  than others on rumors about his health [2]. In the United States, firms whose directors were
  connected to a narrowly elected governor received more state contracts [3]. If connections pay
  off, mapping them shows where the payoffs are likely to be.
- **It tells you where to look.** Investigators have limited time. A map points to the official
  whose brother sits on the board of a company he regulates, so attention goes there first.
- **It runs on public data.** No leaks or subpoena powers are needed, so journalists, researchers
  and ordinary citizens can do it.
- **It catches what money trails miss.** Conflicts of interest, appointments and legal favoritism
  often involve no suspicious payment at all.
- **It makes the money easier to find later.** Once you know the network, you know whose companies,
  contracts and accounts to check. ICIJ used a graph of this kind to investigate the Panama Papers,
  linking companies and individuals [4].

## The limit

A connection is a red flag, not proof. Many ties are innocent, and in a small country the elite
inevitably know each other. Being related, or having gone to the same school, is not wrongdoing.
The map tells you where to dig; documents and money trails supply the evidence. VIP is a lead and
a tool for investigative journalism, and it aggregates what reputable media and official registries
report.
